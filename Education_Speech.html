Education: What Are We Performing For?

A Speech on Education, Learning and the Purpose Behind It

Good morning, respected teachers and my dear friends.

Today, I would like to speak about something students wonder every day: whether the education system we know is still fulfilling the purpose for which it was created. And if it is, then what exactly are we performing for?

We are often told that education should make us knowledgeable, creative, disciplined and capable of thinking independently. And ideally, that is exactly what a school should do. But somewhere between the textbook and the timetable, education can sometimes become more concerned with completion than comprehension, and performance than curiosity.

Assignments, worksheets, homework and projects are important. Practice is necessary, and discipline certainly has its place. But when several pieces of work arrive together, often with similar questions and the same basic level of practice, the result can become rather chaotic. A student may spend hours completing what has been assigned, while having very little time left for self-study, research or simply exploring something out of curiosity.

And that creates an interesting contradiction. We ask students to develop critical thinking, yet sometimes leave them so occupied with prescribed work that they have little opportunity to think beyond it.

A full notebook may show that work has been completed. It does not necessarily show that understanding has taken place.

The same question arises with our approach to marks. Examinations are necessary. They help us measure progress and identify areas that need improvement. But when marks become the most visible measure of success, students can gradually begin preparing for the examination rather than preparing for the world beyond it.

This becomes particularly important for senior students. Alongside school, many have coaching, competitive examinations, mock tests and independent preparation to manage. Perhaps schools could therefore offer a little more flexibility to senior students in managing these responsibilities. Flexibility does not mean lowering standards. It can mean giving students the responsibility to organize their own learning while still meeting those standards.

There is also the question of discipline.

Students need discipline, but discipline does not have to depend upon fear. A classroom should be a place where a student can say, “I do not understand,” without being afraid of humiliation. Mistakes should be corrected, not treated as crimes.

And physical punishment makes this question even more serious. Despite legal and regulatory safeguards against physical punishment and mental harassment, such practices can still occur. We should perhaps ask ourselves whether fear can really produce the kind of respect that education is supposed to teach.

Teachers themselves work under considerable pressure, and students do as well. Perhaps the answer is not fewer expectations, but better priorities.

Because the world outside school will not always give us a worksheet with numbered questions, a textbook with highlighted answers, or an examination paper telling us exactly what is expected.

It will give us unfamiliar problems.

And perhaps that is where we should measure the success of education: not simply by how well a student completes what has been given, but by how confidently they can think, question, learn and solve what has never been given to them before.

So perhaps the real purpose of education is not merely to prepare students to pass examinations.

Perhaps it is to prepare them for a world in which there are no answer keys.

Thank you.
